The Effects

Questions We Never Think to Ask

Why do we not even consider so many obvious questions about the world?

By Johnny B. Quacker

It’s simple: this is the water we swim in, every minute of every day. We don’t even notice it, because it is, and always has been, just there. How did “it” get there at all, when it was new and strange and didn’t make any sense?

Start with two questions, and then ask more questions about the supposedly true answers.

Why is flour “enriched”?

Why is salt “iodized”?

Baffle With BS

Use special jargon.
“Silicon dioxide” instead of “sand.”
Refer to mysterious ideas as fact.
“This is scientifically formulated!”
State a half-truth and move on quickly to another distraction.
“Gluttony is, of course, the cause of fatness.”
Threaten with vague causes of specific fears.
“If you don’t eat scientific food, you will die of a heart attack.”
Shift blame to disobeying the dogma.
“Obviously, your heart attack is because you secretly had red meat.”
Ignore and wave off evidence.
“All across the world, people are sicker and fatter by their own choices.”

These tactics always, always, always work, and they’ve been used for all of human history. The new problem is applying them to the most essential thing we do.

Eating is essential. What we eat is, very literally, what we use to build our bodies. If a baby doesn’t eat, it doesn’t grow into a child. The difference in body size is the food that stayed and became the body.

Baffling on this one subject is the most damaging thing that can be done to a population.

Really think about that. Baffling on politics, religion, society or economics can certainly produce misery and strife. But it doesn’t weaken, sicken and slowly kill off an entire population, for generations.

Generations? Really?

Yes, beyond economics and culture and beliefs that endure. Don’t shut down your brain, but there is a field of study called epigenetics. Literally, “on top of genes.” Or, in plain language: effects on your body and chemistry that are still present because of the physical experiences of your ancestors.

If your grandparents (both sexes) experienced famine, your body chemistry (your “blood”) remembers it, at least slightly. This field explains how the family blood “remembers”, by reference to enzymes and such that influence how the actual DNA behaves.

Yes, generations are affected by bad food. Go down the rabbit hole yourself if you’re intrigued, but be careful about the credibility of the original sources.
