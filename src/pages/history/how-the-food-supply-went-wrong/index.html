The History

How the American Food Supply Went Wrong

A century of well-meant laws, quiet loopholes and cheap calories — and how they built the supermarket you shop in today.

The In-Brief
  • The first food laws were about obvious fraud and filth. They worked.
  • Since 1958, many new ingredients have reached shelves as “generally recognized as safe” — often on the company’s own say-so.
  • Farm policy from the 1970s on rewarded growing as much corn and soy as possible.
  • Official advice to cut fat led to a wave of low-fat foods, many with added sugar.
1906

The First Rules

In 1906, Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle showed readers what went on inside Chicago’s meatpacking plants. The same year Congress passed the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act — the first federal rules on what could be sold as food. Their target was plain: rotten meat, fake ingredients, labels that lied.

1958

The GRAS Door

The Food Additives Amendment of 1958 said new additives needed FDA approval before going on sale. But it made an exception for substances “generally recognized as safe” by qualified experts. Today a company can decide its own ingredient is GRAS, and it is not required to tell the FDA.

1970s

Cheap Corn

In the 1970s, federal farm policy pushed farmers to plant “fencerow to fencerow” and maximise output. Corn and soy became cheap raw materials, and high-fructose corn syrup entered the food supply in the same decade.

1977–92

The Low-Fat Era

A 1977 Senate committee report, Dietary Goals for the United States, urged Americans to eat less fat. The first Dietary Guidelines followed in 1980, and the 1992 Food Guide Pyramid put bread, cereal, rice and pasta at its base. Food makers answered with low-fat versions of nearly everything — many of them sweeter than before.
